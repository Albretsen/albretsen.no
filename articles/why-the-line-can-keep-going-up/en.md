# Why the line can keep going up

In July 1946, prices in Hungary doubled about every fifteen hours. The central bank issued a banknote for one hundred quintillion pengő — a one followed by twenty zeros. On the first of August the forint arrived, at four hundred octillion pengő to the forint. Twenty-nine zeros, deleted in a morning.

The episode is useful precisely because it is absurd. Nothing in Hungary became 10²⁹ times more valuable that summer. The ruler had broken, and someone replaced the ruler. Hold on to that distinction. This essay is about a question that sounds naive and is not: can an economy keep growing forever?

[The previous essay](/write/stop-caring-about-all-time-highs/) argued that a stock market at a record high is a rising line doing what rising lines do. This one asks why the line rises at all, and whether there is any good reason to expect it to stop.

## Two lines, not one

When someone says the economy is growing, they are describing two different lines. The first is nominal: output counted in today's money. The second is real: the same output with inflation taken out. Most of the confusion about "infinite growth" comes from mixing them up.

The nominal line goes up by design. Since 2012 the Federal Reserve has stated that two percent inflation is the goal; Norway set a target of 2.5 percent in 2001 and lowered it to 2 percent in 2018. A two percent target is a promise that prices will double roughly every 35 years and rise about sevenfold in a century. So nominal GDP is supposed to set records even when nothing real changes, and it does: since 1947, US nominal GDP has hit a new all-time high in about nine quarters out of ten.

Over a century this compounds. Since 1913 the dollar has lost about 97 percent of its purchasing power. What cost one krone in Norway in 1913 costs roughly seventy kroner today. Nobody is seventy times richer because of that. The ruler shrank.

> Big numbers are a property of the ruler, not of the wealth.

The real line is the interesting one, and it gets its own section below. But when the nominal line moves fast enough, something physical has to give.

## When the numbers outgrow the ruler

When prices rise fast enough, the numbers stop fitting their containers — banknotes, ledgers, cash registers, eventually software — and someone has to raise the ceiling. The usual way is to lop off zeros.

Germany did it first at scale. In 1914 a dollar cost 4.2 marks. By November 1923 it cost 4.2 trillion, and the largest note was for a hundred trillion marks. The fix was the Rentenmark, at one trillion old marks to one — a rate chosen because it restored the pre-war 4.2 per dollar exactly. The only change was deleting twelve zeros.

Zimbabwe did it three times in three years: three zeros in 2006, ten in 2008 and twelve in 2009. The 2008 cut came with an unusually honest explanation: the Associated Press reported that the computers, calculators and cash machines at the country's banks could no longer handle basic transactions in billions and trillions. The hundred-trillion-dollar note it issued six months later now sells to tourists for a few dollars, more than it was ever worth as money. Venezuela, Turkey, Brazil and even Iceland have done the same.

Software has its own version. In December 2014 a K-pop video approached 2,147,483,647 views, the largest number a signed 32-bit integer can hold. YouTube's counter moved to 64 bits, which raised the ceiling to about 9.2 quintillion. The fix is Hungary's, run in reverse. Instead of making the number smaller, you make the box bigger.

The United States has written one of these ceilings into law. The first general limit on federal debt, in 1939, was 45 billion dollars. Since July 2025 it has been 41.1 trillion. A fixed number of dollars in an economy whose dollar size is designed to grow every year will keep hitting its head. It is reported as a crisis each time; really it is a ruler being lengthened to fit the thing it measures.

Norway's version is quieter. The krone has never needed a redenomination, but the same pressure showed up at the bottom of the scale. The 1- and 2-øre coins lost legal status in 1975, the 5 and 25 øre in 1985, the 10 øre in 1993 and the 50 øre in 2012. A low-inflation economy does not raise the ceiling. It lets the floor fall away.

None of this is growth. Deleting zeros, widening a counter, raising a legal limit: each changes the unit and leaves the economy exactly where it was.

## Where real growth comes from

Take the inflation out and the line still rises. By the Maddison Project's reconstruction (Bolt and van Zanden, 2024), the average person in 1820 lived on about 1,100 dollars a year in 2011 prices, roughly what people had lived on for most of recorded history. In 2022 the figure was about 16,700, nearly fifteen times higher. Something changed.

The first good account of what did not change it came from Robert Solow (1956). Piling up machines cannot sustain growth by itself, because each extra machine adds less than the last; saving more raises the level of income, not its long-run growth rate. His accounting for the United States a year later found that about seven-eighths of the doubling in output per hour between 1909 and 1949 came from the leftover he called technical change. The engine was in the residual.

Paul Romer (1990) explained what the residual is made of. Ideas are non-rival: if I use a recipe, you can use it too, and so can everyone else, at no extra cost. That is how an economy built on ideas escapes Solow's diminishing returns. Romer's picture is a kitchen: valuable things are made by combining cheap ingredients according to a recipe, and growth comes from better recipes rather than more cooking. His back-of-the-envelope sum: with about a hundred elements in the periodic table there are roughly 94 million ways to choose four, and with a few thousand sets of proportions for each, about 330 billion recipes. In his words, "possibilities do not merely add up; they multiply."

William Nordhaus, who shared Romer's 2018 Nobel, supplied the clearest single example of what a better recipe does to a price. His history of lighting (1997) estimated that in Babylon around 1750 BC, some 41 hours of labour bought a thousand lumen-hours of light. By 1992, with a compact fluorescent bulb, it took about half a second. An hour's work buys roughly 350,000 times as much light.

## But the planet is finite

The Limits to Growth (Meadows, Meadows, Randers and Behrens, 1972) is usually misremembered as a prediction of collapse by the year 2000. Its actual claim was conditional: if the growth trends of 1970 continued unchanged, planetary limits would be reached within a century, most likely followed by a sudden decline in population and industry. It also said the trends could be changed. Later checks against the data (Turner, 2008; Herrington, 2021) cannot yet tell a pollution-driven collapse scenario from one in which growth halts without collapse; the two only part ways after about 2020.

Robert Gordon (2016) makes a different pessimistic case: the century from 1870 to 1970 was a one-off. His projection for US output per person from 2015 to 2040 is about 0.8 percent a year. That is a rising line, just a flatter one. Even the leading pessimist is not forecasting a plateau.

Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb (2020, "Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?") ask whether the easy recipes are used up. The United States has had to double its research effort roughly every thirteen years just to keep growth per person steady. Whether that effort can keep growing is a real and open question.

The reply to all of this is that GDP measures value, not tonnes. The model behind The Limits to Growth tracks physical stocks, which is exactly the thing Solow and Romer say the growth is not made of. Andrew McAfee (2019, More from Less) went through the 72 materials the US Geological Survey tracks and found that American consumption of all but six had peaked and was falling, in absolute terms, while the economy kept growing. Global material use is still rising, so that is a proof of possibility, not a victory lap. Carbon tells the same story. Between 2005 and 2019, 32 countries grew their economies while cutting CO₂ emissions, even counting the emissions in what they import (Hausfather, 2021). The link between growth and carbon is breakable, and in a few dozen countries it has already broken.

> The planet is finite. The list of recipes is not.

Ecological limits are real. They constrain how we grow — what we burn, what we dig up, what we put in the air. They do not show that the line must flatten, because the line is not counting the things they constrain.

## What this means for the line on a chart

An index is a claim on earnings, and earnings are a slice of the economy. If the economy grows, nominally by design and really by invention, the earnings grow, and so does the thing that is a claim on them.

The long-run data agree. Dimson, Marsh and Staunton put world equities at about 5.2 percent a year after inflation through 2024, against 1.7 percent for bonds. For the United States the figure is around 6.5 percent; Jeremy Siegel (Stocks for the Long Run, 2022) gets about 6.7 percent real going back to 1802. Earnings grow a little more slowly than GDP, because new companies capture a share of the growth that existing shareholders never see. Bernstein and Arnott (2003) put that gap at about two points a year.

That drift, nominal by construction and real by ideas, is the thing underneath the all-time highs in the [previous essay](/write/stop-caring-about-all-time-highs/). The records are not the market being clever. They are the line being a line.

## The honest caveats

- Growth is a tendency, not a promise. The S&P went from September 1929 to September 1954 without a new nominal high. Twenty-five years is longer than most people's patience and some people's retirement.
- Real growth may well slow. Gordon's 0.8 percent and Bloom's doubling-every-thirteen-years are serious arguments, not strawmen.
- Nominal growth is not wealth. Seventy kroner for a 1913 krone is a fact about the ruler.

## So

Two lines. The nominal one rises because central banks have decided it should, and when it rises too fast the zeros get deleted, the counters get widened and the ceilings get raised, none of which makes anyone richer or poorer. The real one rises because people keep finding better recipes, and a recipe, unlike a tonne of copper, can be used by everyone at once.

Whether the planet can support infinite growth is the wrong question, because growth is not measured in planet. The right question is whether we keep finding recipes faster than the old ones run out. For two centuries the answer has been yes. That is not a guarantee. It is a very long streak with a mechanism behind it.
